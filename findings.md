## Summary of what was found

- **11 of 15** candidate patterns survived confirmation on the held-out half, kept their significance after the false-discovery-rate correction, **and** held a stable effect across bootstrap resamples.
- **The dominant driver is dissonance, or roughness**: progressions containing **fewer clashing note-pairs** — notes a semitone or less apart, sounding together — are reliably **liked**, while clash-heavy ones are **disliked**. This is the strongest signal found, and it comes from pure subtraction between note values, with no music theory imposed.
- Secondary, weaker signals: **smaller jumps between chords and a calmer top note**, and a **higher-pitched final chord**.
- **There is real signal.** A classifier separating liked from not-liked scores **0.68** where 0.50 is pure chance, against a shuffled-rating baseline of 0.50 (p = 0.032). Modest, but above chance.
- Effect sizes per pattern remain **small** (about 0.12 to 0.19 on the −1 to +1 scale): useful as *tendencies*, not as a confident per-progression predictor.

**Combined conditions mostly add up rather than multiply.** A formal test for interaction (comparing a model with a combined term against one without) found **no significant interaction** for the pairs tested — a two-part pattern raises the average rating by stacking two independent effects (less dissonance plus calmer movement between chords), not through genuine synergy. The practical takeaway is simply: **several weak signals accumulate.**


## What the musical quantities mean

- **Semitone clashes** — how many pairs of notes sounding together sit one semitone apart. Fewer clashes means less dissonance, which is the strongest single predictor of being liked.
- **Near-unison clashes** — pairs of notes less than a semitone apart, close enough to beat against each other. Fewer is liked.
- **Tightest interval** — the smallest gap between any two notes anywhere in the progression; a very small gap sounds harsh.
- **Biggest single jump** — the largest distance any one voice moves when the chord changes. Smaller jumps make a smoother progression.
- **Top-note movement** — how far the highest note travels from chord to chord; less movement reads as calmer.
- **Final-chord height** — the pitch of the notes in the last chord; higher means a higher-pitched ending.
