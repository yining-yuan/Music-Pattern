# Preference patterns in the reshuffled sets — findings

_Generated by `Music-Pattern-Analysis/discover_reshuffled.py` with a fixed random seed, so the numbers reproduce exactly._

## Read this first: the five sets are not five ratings

The five files hold the **same 99 chords** with **identical notes**, reshuffled into five different presentation orders (median cross-set order correlation +0.040 — the orders really are independent). But the preference column is the **same in all five**: 99 of 99 chords carry an identical rating across every set, giving a mean pairwise agreement of 1.000. Those ratings also match the labels already in `Data/tabulated_chords.csv` for the same chords (100% agreement).

**Consequence.** `pref_trial1` … `pref_trial5` are five copies of one judgement, not five re-ratings. Test–retest reliability, rater consistency and a directly measured noise ceiling — the things repeated presentations exist to provide — **cannot be estimated from this data**. If the intention was to re-rate the same chords in a fresh order, the answers appear to have been carried over from the first session rather than re-collected.

Everything below therefore treats preference as **one label per chord**.

## What the design does still give us

The 99 chords are a deliberately **balanced** subset — exactly 33 like, 33 not sure, 33 dislike — drawn from the 800 chords in `tabulated_chords.csv`. That makes them a clean discovery sample (no class is rare), and it leaves **701 chords that appear in none of the reshuffled sets** as a genuinely disjoint, well-powered confirmation set. Patterns are found on the 99 and tested on those 701, so nothing is believed on the data that produced it.

## Statistical methods used — and why

**1. Preference treated as ordered, not as three categories.** Dislike, not sure and like are scored −1, 0, +1. *Why:* it lets us ask the stronger question — does a pattern move the rating steadily in one direction — instead of merely whether it is more common in one bucket. Whether the ordering is real is checked, not assumed.

**2. Every musical quantity is pure arithmetic on the note numbers.** Intervals, spreads, movement, clash counts and melodic shape come from adding and subtracting note values. *Why:* no music-theory categories such as "major" or "cadence" are imposed, so any finding is a fact about the numbers rather than an artefact of the labels we chose to compute.

**3. Discover on the 99, confirm on the 701 chords never in these files.** *Why:* this is the central safeguard. Splitting 99 chords in half would leave roughly 40 for testing — too few to confirm anything. Using the untouched remainder of the parent dataset gives a confirmation set seven times larger than the discovery set and completely disjoint from it.

**4. Rank correlation for the initial screen.** *Why:* rank methods assume only that a relationship is consistently increasing or decreasing — not that it is a straight line, and not that the values are normally distributed. Pitch data is bounded and lumpy, so this is the safe choice.

**5. Subgroup discovery to find pockets, not just trends.** A search over threshold conditions and their pairs, scored by how far a subgroup's average rating departs from the overall average, weighted by subgroup size. *Why:* a quantity can be useless overall yet decisive within a region; weighting by size stops the search chasing freak subgroups of three or four chords.

**6. Gradient-boosted trees as a cross-check.** *Why:* they catch curved relationships and interactions a rank correlation would miss. Used only to *nominate* candidates, never as evidence on their own.

**7. Permutation tests for confirmation.** Ratings on the held-out chords are reshuffled five thousand times to build the distribution of effects expected from chance alone. *Why:* it assumes nothing whatsoever about the shape of the data.

**8. Effect size alongside significance (Cliff's delta).** How often chords matching a pattern are rated above chords that do not, from −1 to +1. *Why:* a small p-value says an effect is unlikely to be zero, not that it is large.

**9. False-discovery-rate correction across all tests (Benjamini–Hochberg).** *Why:* testing many patterns at the usual 5% threshold lets roughly 1 in 20 pure-noise patterns through.

**10. Bootstrap confidence intervals for stability.** Each survivor is re-measured on two thousand resamples. *Why:* a pattern whose interval straddles zero is riding on a handful of influential chords.

**11. A base-rate caveat, stated rather than hidden.** The 99 are forced to 33/33/33 while the held-out 701 are 61% dislike. *Why it matters:* effect *sizes* are not directly comparable between the two sets, so the discovery and held-out columns are reported side by side and judged on **direction and significance**, not on magnitude. A secondary column repeats each test on the held-out chords whose ids fall in the same range as the 99, in case the rater drifted over the session.

**How to read the tables.** *Effect size* runs from −1 to +1; positive means the pattern is rated higher than the rest, and roughly 0.1 is small, 0.3 moderate. The *95% interval* is the plausible range for that effect. *p-value* is the chance of an effect this large if the pattern were pure noise. *Number matching* is how many held-out chords the pattern covers.

## Summary of what was found

- **13 of 20** candidate patterns survived confirmation on the held-out chords, kept their significance after the false-discovery-rate correction, and held a stable effect across bootstrap resamples.
- **The musical quantities that matter reproduce across the two disjoint sets.** Ranking every quantity by its association with preference in the 99 and again in the 701 gives two rankings correlated at **+0.28** (p = 4.7e-03), and the twenty strongest quantities point the same way **90%** of the time. The signal is a property of the music, not of which chords happened to be sampled.
- **There is real signal.** A classifier separating liked from not-liked scores **0.68** on the held-out chords where 0.50 is pure chance, against a shuffled-rating baseline of 0.50 (p = 0.005). Inside the 99 chords alone the same check scores 0.55 against 0.51 (p = 0.305) — **not** significant, which is what 99 chords buys you rather than evidence against the signal: the effect is real but small, and detecting a small effect needs hundreds of items. This is precisely why the patterns are confirmed on the 701.
- Effect sizes per pattern stay **small**: these are tendencies in taste, not a confident per-chord predictor.

## Checks on whether the analysis itself is trustworthy

**Is there any signal at all?** On the held-out chords, separating liked from not-liked scores **0.676** where 0.50 is chance and 1.00 perfect; the same method on shuffled ratings scores 0.501 (p = 0.005). There **is** detectable signal above chance — but it is small.

**Does 'not sure' really sit between dislike and like?** Placing all three groups on one dislike-to-like scale gives dislike -2.70, not sure +0.07, like +2.88 → 'not sure' **does** fall between the other two, so treating preference as an ordered scale is **supported**.

**Do the findings survive changing the chords?** The association of each quantity with preference, measured independently in the 99 and in the 701, correlates at +0.283 (p = 4.7e-03). This is the strongest available evidence that the patterns are about the music rather than about the sample.

**Does the actual octave matter, or only which pitch it is?** Measured on the 701 held-out chords, using exact pitches scores 0.581 against 0.576 when octaves are folded together → which pitch it is matters, but not which octave.

**What this data cannot tell us.** Because the five sets repeat one set of answers, there is no way to measure how consistent the rater is with themselves, and therefore no directly measured ceiling on how well any method could do. Collecting genuinely independent re-ratings of these same 99 chords would supply exactly that, and would also let a consensus score be built that is far less noisy than a single judgement.
